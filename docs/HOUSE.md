# The house: what Watermark is, and what hangs here

Will's words, 17 October 2026, kept as the standard. Drafts in his voice until he confirms them.

## What it is
- The digital Met: the house holds the object, not a picture of it (the archival master, fixity, the register, the papers).
- The digital Magnum: a curated house of artists with a platform, in the tradition of Cartier-Bresson. By invitation. It is an honour to hang here; you have to work for it. Not a place anyone uploads from a phone.
- The Library: books, journals, essays, papers, important digital copies; art history and art culture. Read in the house for nothing; pay to take or use. The Library of Alexandria: a shelf of work that is free and clear (public domain, Creative Commons), beautiful imagery anyone may use; and the museums' portal: a museum can put its whole collection here, the open part open, the rest licensed at the source. (To build: an `open` rule on works — pd, cc0, cc-by — shown as a shelf, taken without a license; institution accounts.)
- The nav stays short. The Library lives inside the Collection.
- A place to wander: StumbleUpon for beautiful things. Curiosity satisfied: learn the work, the maker, their other media (writing, film, music).
- The essential studio tool: the whole of an artist's working life (the vault, the register, licenses, contracts, requests, taxes, the Copyright Office), so what is left is the work.
- A walled garden. No advertising, ever. Kept by members and the friends of the house.

## Made for the public (Will, 18 October)
- A place the curious can come and feel fed and seen, and told: you're allowed to look around; we built this place for you to enjoy; this hall is dedicated to you. In the tradition of the great public buildings (Grand Central): the most beautiful place made for the public. A gift for people, a gift for artists, and a tool to be used; the quality of the tool is in whether people use it. Our work is to build the frame for what is possible.
- Two people, two kinds of knowledge, one house, each to their delight: the art-world professional and the one who comes in to look. The museum serves both. The stock house (Getty) serves a purpose, but no one wanders there; Watermark carries a Getty's working side almost invisibly (For the trade: the spec, every use and its price, comps, lightboxes, quotes), switched on by those who need it, never laid over the picture for those who came to look.

## What hangs here
- Work worth presenting, at the level of thought of the Met. Nothing pornographic. A nude is a thoughtful nude or it does not hang.
- The beautiful rises (regard); the body of work is kept whole; nothing is deleted by taste.
- No identifiable people without consent.

## The team to build
Museum-grade, editorial: curators, art historians, people from the auction houses (Sotheby's) and the great archives. The house's judgement is human; the AI guides (Look closer) are labelled and never the last word.

## What a watermark is (Will, 18 October)

The old watermark is a stamp across a picture that says *keep out*. Ours is the opposite: it is what the picture carries. Who made it, its number, who holds the copyright, how the maker is paid, who holds it now and under what license, how anyone can have it, and the proof. It travels inside every file the house sends out, and anyone can read it.

The aim is that the word comes to mean this, the way a brand name becomes the name of the thing. To watermark a photograph is to put it in the house: held, numbered, registered, and paid for when it is used. "Is it watermarked?" should mean "can I see who made it and how to pay them?"

In the house this is the eye: on every photograph, a quiet glyph top right. It opens *The watermark*, the information the picture carries. The picture itself stays clean; the marks on the plates are ghosts until you reach for them. No K on Watermark's own pages; the K stays with Krafft's sites.

Draft definition, for Will to confirm or rewrite: **watermark** (n.), the information a photograph carries: who made it, who may use it, and how the maker is paid. (v.), to place a photograph in Watermark so it carries that information wherever it goes.

The domain watermark.com is Will's to pursue; nothing is bought from here.

## Staying is what pays (Will, 18 October)

Social media made looking into a feed: a scroll, a swipe, a comment, in your face. The house borrows the swipe because that is how a thumb moves on a phone, but it must not borrow the bargain. A feed pays you for moving on; Watermark pays you for staying.

So in the stroll: hold still and everything but the photograph falls away. Stay longer and one line of the guide rises, as a docent might lean in. Racing on earns nothing; once, after a fast run, the next photograph holds and says "Stay with this one." There are no numbers on the wall: the tip of the hat is given, never counted where the visitor can see it. And there is Sit: each photograph held a minute and a half, the guide read aloud, quiet music made in the room, nothing to press. Someone could walk through the house half-asleep and come out full.

## Turning analog (Will, 18 October)
The collector is who most people here will be, so what they hold must be the best room in the house, and they must be given everything to use it. Above all: print it, and see it in person. The holder's room (/yours/p/:ref) shows the sizes their file prints at, gives the file, teaches them to print it themselves (/print, the house's guide), or takes an order for the house to print it. The print room is by hand for now (`print_orders`, mailed to the house); a printing business, ours or a partner lab's, is the plan.

The Frick is the model. The Krafft Collection is to be what the Frick is: a collection lived with in a house, not a white cube. One day a Gilded Age house on the Upper East Side, near the Met, where the analog work is printed and hung, and Watermark is also a small physical place. (Will's ambition, private until he says otherwise; not public copy.)

## Wander is the house (Will, 18 October)
Wander is what makes the house bingeable; the rest serves it. The front door, the header and the line beneath the hero all lead there with one button, the wave and the word. Inside it: Find (straight to the picture) and Settings (the guide, the pace, the sound), and nothing else in the way. A phone turned sideways shows a horizontal work full screen, the way Instagram never does.

## Straight to the picture (Will, 18 October)
Finding art is always hard; the long walk up to the one you came for. Find is one search for the whole house, the same everywhere (the header, the stroll, / or ⌘K, /find): as you type, the works themselves appear, with their pictures; Enter opens the first; a slip of the fingers is forgiven; before you type, where you stood lately and what is yours, then the ways in. A word with no single work wanders through everything that matches.

## Unreasonable hospitality (Will, 19 October)

Everything the house does is operated as hospitality, at the level of a three-star room and past it. The standard is Thomas Keller's: there is no such thing as perfection in food, and seeking it anyway is the point, because the point is to make people happy. That is what Watermark and Krafft Studios are for: to make people happy; to make beautiful places for beautiful work to be seen in; and for the people who make it to be paid.

So every interaction of the house says, in its own words, four things: thank you; you are our honoured guest; this is our house and our home, and you are welcome in it; we hope you come back. The restaurant said it first, on the back of every menu: "It is my extreme pleasure to welcome you among our honored guests." Mary Helen Krafft, 1968. The house's standard since.

Where it is said today: the welcome when you sign in; the letter when you collect; the thanks page; the door out ("Thank you for coming. We hope you come back."); the epigraph on the Keys. The test for any new page or letter: does it thank, welcome, and hope to see them again, without saying so twice.

## The rooms of the Keys (Will, 19 October)

The Keys is the club. It is open all day and all night, all over the world, so it is built as rooms, each curated (a host, a wall, a programme), some kept for certain hours. The look is the restaurant's red made deeper, velvet and gilt and mahogany, after the Morgan's library: Gilded Age beauty with a modern hand.

The rooms today: the Bar Lounge (the benches), the Champagne Room (the balcony, evenings), the Library, the Viewing Room, the Print Room, the Keyhole Room (the dinner). Two are built for a day the house has not reached, and their doors say so:

**The Listening Room.** Hi-fi. The raw master of a recording, played as it was made; vinyl when the house presses it.

**The Screening Room.** Not Netflix. A private screening: films chosen by the people who make them and the people who restore them (the aim is the filmmakers' own lists, and partnerships with the restorers and the archives, none of which is to be claimed in public copy until signed), shown whole, at the highest resolution the master allows, in surround sound, to a home theatre, the way the Paris or the Metrograph would show them. It plays through, as a cinema does; a pause opens the filmmaker's notes.

And the rooms reach into people's own: /enjoy says how to get more from the work in the real world (a television as a wall, good speakers, Atmos and Vision when the masters come, a headset, paper, in person). The house sells none of it; it only says what helps.

## Meeting people (19 Oct)

Why does anyone go to the late night at the museum, or want the Apollo Club's gala? To be among people like them, in a beautiful room, with something worth talking about in front of them. The work gives the reason; the house gives the room; the club gives the people. That is the whole of the social side of Watermark, and it is the thing that is distinct: not a feed, a club, with a calendar, in the real world.

Three things, in `src/season.js`:

- **The season.** The nights the house throws, with Krafft Studios filming: an opening, a late night in the galleries, the dinner at one table, a salon in a maker's own words, a screening, a walk through a city with the photographer who made its pictures. Each night is a page (the picture, the words, the artists present, the reply); the address comes with the reply; who is coming is shown only to those coming, by first name and by what they collect or make. After, the night has its bench, and the house says only how many came.
- **The Club.** Our Apollo Club, in the manner of the Keys: the young members, under forty, by the year. Every night first; the dinner; the artists; the introductions; a key. Dues are a membership, not a gift. The aim is the coolest room in the house, and it is the people.
- **The card.** How two people meet. Over a work, a line or a night, a keyholder leaves a card for another: one sentence, under their name, at most one a day, never the same person twice. If the other says yes, the house introduces them both by letter, at once, and steps back. A no is never told to anyone. Nothing of it on the wall or the register.

Why it is not disgusting, and better than the apps: you meet over the work, under your name, in a sentence, and only when both say yes; the house asks for no photograph of you (the picture on every page is a work); the meeting is meant to end in a room. Discretion: the house never says who came or who holds what outside the room.

## Two floors

Everyone may put work in the day they make it. The record keeps it, fingerprints it, deeds it; it is the frame on your own wall, your work treated as worth making, and the house celebrates it. The walls of the house are a museum's, and hanging there is by invitation. The stock agencies once made a living possible for anyone whose work was good enough; Watermark means to be that again, and the moment is read from the work, not asked for: a body of work, an edit the maker made, professional files, a page and prices. Then the house looks at the edit, large, and hangs the first show itself or writes back in words. The steps are a discipline whether or not the house ever calls: title it, place it, date it, grade it, edit it. What wanders is the edit, so the halls hold up.

## The vault and the jewel box

The house is the container for the masterpieces of the world, so it holds itself to a museum's standard of presentation: nothing the house does may corrupt or diminish a work. The charter, as the code keeps it:

- The original is kept byte for byte, fingerprinted on the register, sealed in the vault, mirrored to a second store, backed up nightly; it is never served.
- Every display copy is made from the original alone: colour-managed to sRGB, never cropped, never enlarged past the original, nothing laid into the pixels, chroma kept whole (4:4:4), quality at or above 88. A wall copy at 3840 px serves screens with more pixels than the large copy; a phone and a 5K display each get a copy at least as sharp as they can show.
- Every change to the pipeline passes the gate (test/pictures.js): a smooth sky and a textured scene through the pipeline as served, within a fifth of a level of block structure of the original and above 45 dB; a Display P3 original comes out sRGB; a truncated original is seen.
- If it is not sound, it is not shown. The condition check runs over the whole archive, a few works at a time, forever: the original decodes whole, matches its fingerprint and its record, and every display copy is judged against the original resized. What fails leaves the walls at once and goes to the restoration room, where it is rebuilt from the original, fetched back from the mirror, or asked of the maker again, and returns only after it passes. Its page says 'Off view for conservation', as a museum's label would; the register says so too. Nothing is ever deleted.
- A browser keeps a display copy for an hour and must ask again; the app's own cache replaces a copy on the next look. A corrected picture reaches every device within the hour.
